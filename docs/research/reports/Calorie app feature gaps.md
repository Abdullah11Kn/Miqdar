# The scale already wins; build around it

Users of calorie apps care most about four things: logging that takes little effort, numbers they can trust, no paywall or ads, and correct portion sizes. Miqdar already does well on three of them. Its grams come from a real scale, not a guess. It is free, with no ads. And it gives away, free, features that rivals charge for: photo, voice and text logging, a doctor's PDF, and carb tools for diabetes. Its leftovers weighing, Ramadan mode, carb warnings before eating and Gulf-food focus did not turn up in any competitor checked. The gaps that matter are narrower than the long feature lists of MyFitnessPal or Cronometer suggest. There are seven of them: one-tap repeats and favourites; a short "how was it cooked" step, because fat and oil are where AI goes wrong once the portion is known; a way to log packaged food from its label; logging from a shared platter (sahn), which no app handles; glucose readings shown beside weighed meals for diabetes users; Apple Health and Health Connect sync; and a weekly summary to share with a dietitian. For daily use, build cooking context, favourites and label reading first. For Saudi and Gulf users, build shared-platter logging and the Arabic-and-English "not for medical use" label and photo consent that Saudi rules point to. For the demonstration, show the shared platter, glucose beside weighed carbs, and a formal accuracy test against the SFDA's new lab-tested Saudi dish tables. Some things should stay out on purpose: insulin dosing, glucose alerts, an AI coach, social features, meal plans and a food database that replaces Gemini. They are regulated, unwanted by users, too large for a student project, or against Abdullah's own decisions.

## Users quit over effort and distrust, not over missing features

The research on why people stop is consistent. Across 18 studies with 525,824 users of lifestyle apps, a median of **70% stopped within 100 days** ([Kidman et al., JMIR 2024](https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e56897)). Among people who quit a nutrition app in a Dutch national survey, the top reason was **"too much time required" (36.8%)**. Focus groups named the same barrier: entering the same products again and again ([van der Haar et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10337335)). That sample was small (38 quitters), but the pattern holds elsewhere. In a 24-week programme, people spent 23 minutes a day logging in the first month and 15 by the sixth. The people who lost at least 10% of their weight logged **2.7 times a day against 1.7**, so how often people logged mattered, not how long ([Harvey et al., Obesity 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30801989/)). The design goal is many quick entries, not a few complete but slow ones.

When the same survey asked people to rate 46 features, the top-rated was **a complete and reliable food database (5.58 out of 7)**. Easy navigation and **few advertisements (5.53)** came next. Only three features scored below neutral: linking to social media, gamification and contact with other users ([van der Haar et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10337335)). App store reviews agree. A study of 72,084 Google Play reviews linked bad ratings to adding new foods, technical faults and charges ([Zečević et al., JMIR 2021](https://www.jmir.org/2021/4/e25160/)). When MyFitnessPal moved its barcode scanner behind the paywall in 2022, users protested loudly and some left for Lose It! ([Droid Life](https://www.droid-life.com/2022/08/24/myfitnesspal-puts-barcode-scanner-behind-premium-paywall/)). Feedback has evidence from trials behind it: meta-analyses find that it raises engagement ([JMIR mHealth 2022](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2022/4/e35479/PDF); [Krukowski & Denton 2024](https://doaj.org/article/eb265c82b7b048eaa77ef83cf41de422)). And 13.2% of quitters said the app had "no new information" to offer.

Saudi evidence adds two things that Western studies leave out. In a Riyadh survey of 1,191 people, 74% of those who had downloaded a weight app had stopped. The top reasons were **no specialist to follow them (26.6%)** and **an app not in their language (24.5%)**, then confusion and hidden costs. The features they wanted most were specialist monitoring, barcode scanning (24 to 28%), nutrition data for many foods, and weekly or monthly progress reports ([Aljuraiban, JMIR mHealth 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6406230/)). The data is from 2018 and nothing newer was found, but language and follow-up from a professional both stand out. The order of user needs that follows from all of this is: low effort, trustworthy values, no paywall or ads, correct portions, useful feedback and goals that can be renewed, food from the user's own region in their own language, freedom to log later, and protection against obsessive use. Social features come last.

## Weighing removes the biggest error; oil and dish identity remain

The best new evidence supports Miqdar's design. In a controlled NIH feeding study, 102 meals were prepared in a metabolic kitchen, and photo apps **underestimated them by 252 to 345 kcal per meal, about 33%**. All four apps underestimated fat by about 30 g. The researchers advised entering amounts by hand ([Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/primary-care/20260804/ai-photobased-calorietracking-tools-underestimate-them-by-33)). That study is a conference abstract, not yet peer reviewed. A peer-reviewed test of ChatGPT-5 on 195 dishes measured how much each extra input helped. A photo alone gave an energy error of **123 kcal**. A photo with non-visual details such as cooking method gave **92 kcal**. A photo with ingredients and amounts gave **53 kcal** ([Rodríguez-Jiménez et al., Nutrients 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12655113/)). Weighing, the hospital DIMS system's before-and-after plate weighing reached a portion agreement (ICC) of 0.99 after eating ([PMC10260788](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10260788/)). In a preprint, Gemini 2.5 Flash's carb error fell from 56.6% to 39.5% when it was given only a *predicted* weight ([Preprints.org](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202605.0623)). A measured weight should do better, but no one has published that test.

So after the scale, what is left is the calorie value per 100 g of a mixed, oily dish, the edible fraction, and the dish's name. Fat is the unstable part, though the studies disagree on its direction. The NIH apps undercounted fat. A study of 10 AI models on hospital meals found that all of them *over*estimated fat by more than 20% ([Isobe et al., Nutrients 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu18060966)). Either way, ghee and oil in kabsa, mandi and saleeg are where Gemini's numbers will drift. Gemini is also the least steady model family on record. In a 26,904-query consistency test, the median spread of carb answers for one photo was about 10 to 11% for Gemini models against 2.4% for Claude. One paella photo drew Gemini 2.5 Pro carb answers from **55 g to 484 g**. The models' confidence scores did not track accuracy, and the dish was misnamed in 8 of 13 images ([Diabettech](https://www.diabettech.com/i-asked-ai-to-count-my-carbs-27000-times)). An older study gave Gemini 1.5 Pro a 64% energy error against about 36% for GPT-4o and Claude 3.5 Sonnet ([Fridolfsson et al. 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12513282/)). Miqdar uses newer Flash models and has no figure of its own yet.

Two lessons follow. First, a quick cooking-context step at capture is the cheapest accuracy gain the evidence offers. It would be a few one-tap choices, such as fried, grilled, with ghee or oil, or with sauce, or a spoken note, sent to Gemini with the photo and grams. SnapCalorie already lets users add a voice note to a photo for this reason ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id1574239307)). Miqdar has "Add Side / Oil / Sauce" and a prompt that assumes the dish's "usual oil". It does not ask, before the analysis, how this particular plate was cooked. Second, the evidence says Miqdar's confidence display tells users nothing useful about how right the carb number is. That is evidence to bring to Abdullah, since he decided the display stays until he removes it. It is not a change to make for him.

## Saudi apps already speak Arabic; none of them weighs a shared platter

Arabic alone no longer sets Miqdar apart. Kilo offers photo scanning ingredient by ingredient, barcodes, 151,000 foods, menus from Albaik, Kudu and Mama Noura, and steps from Apple Health ([Kilo](https://kiloappsa.com/)). Loqma estimates from text and calls itself Gulf-first ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/loqma-ai-calorie-tracker/id6779627300)). Miran has data for Saudi restaurants ([Miran](https://miranapp.com/)), and Tabaq works in Arabic, English and Turkish ([Tabaq](https://tabaq.ai/en/)). All of these claims come from the apps themselves. None of the local apps checked mentions a kitchen scale, Ramadan mode or diabetes mode. Those three, together with home-cooked rather than restaurant food, are Miqdar's real local edge.

The need is large. The IDF puts adult diabetes in Saudi Arabia at **23.1%**, a modelled figure that includes undiagnosed cases ([IDF](https://idf.org/our-network/regions-and-members/middle-east-and-north-africa/members/saudi-arabia/)). A 2025 meta-analysis puts prediabetes at **24.1%** ([PeerJ 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12374689/)). Together that is close to half of Saudi adults, so carb-first display serves a large group, not a niche. Ramadan changes eating sharply. Saudi adults with type 2 diabetes ate significantly more calories and carbohydrate during the fast ([PubMed 38192134](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38192134/)), and glucose monitors show sharp rises after iftar and after suhoor ([PMC7261298](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7261298)). This supports the Ramadan mode already built.

The clearest unserved Gulf habit is eating from a shared dish. Dietary research says standard methods miss what each person eats from a common plate. It recommends photographing or weighing the shared dish before and after, and weighing the staple food, because rice and bread carry most of the error ([Burrows et al., Nutrients 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6520825/)). Researchers working with Negev Bedouin families had to adapt the 24-hour recall for meals eaten straight from a large shared dish ([Public Health Nutrition](https://cambridge.org/core/journals/public-health-nutrition/article/assessing-individual-dietary-intake-from-commonplate-meals-a-new-tool-for-an-enduring-practice/6B62C70997D80027B2C97BA13DB239B7)). Weighing before and after eating is called the most accurate intake method, though too much work for large surveys ([Frontiers in Nutrition 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnut.2024.1505606/full)). Miqdar already does it for one plate with Weigh What's Left. Extending it to a platter fits the hardware and meets a documented gap, and would be striking to show. How many Gulf households eat this way each day was not measured in any source found.

Saudi Arabia now also has a reference that did not exist two years ago. On 25 June 2026 the SFDA published the **Saudi Food Composition Tables**: 130 Saudi dishes, each prepared three times, with 49 nutrients from more than 19,000 lab analyses, free as a PDF ([SFDA](https://www.sfda.gov.sa/en/news/19089); [SFCT PDF](https://www.sfda.gov.sa/sites/default/files/2026-04/SFCT-E.pdf)). The tables could be used as a check on Gemini's values, without replacing Gemini, which keeps Abdullah's rule. They would make the formal accuracy test much more credible. Saudi diners also already know the SFDA's menu icon for items over 5 g of salt, required since July 2025 ([SFDA](https://www.sfda.gov.sa/en/news/17639)).

Privacy matters here more than usual. Users of the MOH's Sehhaty app named privacy and transparency about data protection as major reasons not to use it ([PMC 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12014757/)). Under the PDPL, health data needs explicit consent, and transfers out of the Kingdom are restricted ([Herbert Smith Freehills](https://www.hsf.com/insights/2023-11/saudi-arabias-personal-data-protection-law-–-what-you-need-to-know); [Clyde & Co](https://www.clydeco.com/en/insights/2025/03/update-on-saudi-arabia-risk-assessment-guidelines)). Sending a food photo to Google's Gemini is such a transfer. Keeping the diary on the phone helps. But no explicit consent step before the first Gemini request was found in the code, and diabetes status should never go into a prompt. Whether food photos count as health data under the PDPL, and how the law treats a student project, were not settled by any source. This is not legal advice.

## Diabetes apps centre on glucose after the meal; Miqdar can show carbs before it

The diabetes apps are built around one view: what a meal did to glucose. Undermyfork pairs each meal photo with glucose from a monitor or meter, read through Apple Health, and colours the meal by time in range over the next two hours ([diaTribe](https://diatribe.org/diabetes-technology/diabetes-food-logging-app-undermyfork-launches-android-users-and-healthcare)). Dexcom added AI photo logging and a "Glucose Impact" view to Stelo and G7 in 2025 ([Dexcom](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250728353105/en/Dexcom-Launches-Revolutionary-AI-Powered-Meal-Logging-Feature-Across-Glucose-Biosensing-Portfolio/)). Abbott Lingo and Gluroo do much the same, and Gluroo lets caregivers follow along ([Gluroo](https://gluroo.com/)). mySugr logs carbs, insulin and glucose and puts its bolus calculator behind a paid tier as a class IIb medical device ([Accu-Chek UK](https://accu-chek.co.uk/mysugr-app)). None of these apps uses a measured weight. All estimate the portion from the image. Miqdar's red warning, given *before* eating ("this plate is above your carb number for this meal"), was not found in any app surveyed.

What diabetes users complain about most is not the food features. It is apps that **fail to connect to devices (25.1% of reviews)** or **lose or lock away their data (22%)** ([Turner & Stawarz, JMIR Diabetes 2025](https://diabetes.jmir.org/2025/1/e62926)). For Miqdar, that makes the reliability of the phone-to-scale link and of saving the diary the most important "diabetes feature". It also means a badly built health sync would hurt more than having none.

The safe line is clear in both the FDA and SFDA texts. The FDA treats calorie counters and dietary suggestions as outside oversight. It puts logging blood glucose "to eventually share with a heath care professional" (the FDA's own spelling) under enforcement discretion. Its focus is on software that calculates doses or "analyzes readings from a blood glucose monitor and alerts the user of readings outside a predetermined range" ([FDA guidance PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/80958/download)). The SFDA's MDS-G27 (August 2025) lists "an app that allows users to log meals and calculate nutritional content for general wellness purposes" as **not** a medical device. An app that recommends insulin dose changes **is** one. It also gives as a medical-device example an epilepsy app that "generates reports for their healthcare providers". And it requires wellness products to say, **in Arabic and English, that they are not intended for medical purposes** ([SFDA MDS-G27](https://www.sfda.gov.sa/sites/default/files/2025-08/MDS-G027.pdf)). The doctor's PDF is already the feature closest to that report example. Its defence is that it is a copy of the user's own log, with no interpretation, and adding glucose to it moves it closer to the line. This reading of the guidance is a judgement, not a ruling. Miqdar is not being sold, so marketing authorization does not apply now. Staying on the wellness side keeps a later Saudi launch simple.

The safe form of the missing glucose feature follows from this. The user types a reading taken before the meal or two hours after, or Miqdar reads it from Apple Health later. The number is shown beside the weighed carbs on Meal Detail and in the report. There is no colour verdict, no alert and no dose suggestion. Undermyfork's green, amber and red colouring sits close to the "alerts outside a range" line, so Miqdar should show the plain number only.

## Features to leave out on purpose

**Insulin dose suggestions, insulin-to-carb ratios and bolus calculators.** Both the FDA and the SFDA treat dose calculation as a medical-device function, and mySugr's calculator is class IIb. The UK Diabetes Technology Network says generic AI must not be used to calculate doses, as reported by [Diabettech](https://www.diabettech.com/i-asked-ai-to-count-my-carbs-27000-times). Gemini's 55 to 484 g spread on one photo shows why.

**Glucose alerts, low-glucose calls, glucose predicted from a photo, and estimated HbA1c.** Analysing readings to alert on out-of-range values is the FDA's own example of oversight ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/media/80958/download)). Prediction and HbA1c estimates turn a log into a medical interpretation.

**An AI chat coach.** MFP, Cronometer and Google Health are adding coaches. But the Saudi demand is for a *human* specialist, Gemini's free quota is already the bottleneck, and free-form health advice is the riskiest thing to put under a wellness label. Google Health's coach-led rebuild drew a user backlash over its food tracker ([9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2026/05/27/google-health-roadmap-fitbit-backlash/)).

**Social features, streaks and gamification.** These were the lowest-rated of 46 features (3.5 to 3.8 out of 7). Of people with an eating disorder who used MyFitnessPal, 73% felt it had contributed to their disorder ([Levinson et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28843591/)). A scale that weighs every gram should not add pressure on top.

**Meal plans, a recipe library and grocery lists.** These are content projects, not engineering. Drop's users complained about how small its recipe library was ([SlashGear](https://www.slashgear.com/drop-scale-review-no-need-to-weigh-your-options-21360086)), and Calo already serves this market with delivered meals. Abdullah also turned down cook-once recipe weighing on 7 November.

**A verified food database or restaurant-chain menus in place of Gemini.** This goes against his decision. Building a Saudi database took the Twazon team 23 months ([JMIR Research Protocols 2016](https://www.researchprotocols.org/2016/2/e76)). Local apps already lead on chains like Albaik and Kudu, and Saudi menus display calories by law, so Enter by Hand covers a restaurant meal.

**Accounts and cloud sync now.** The honesty rules forbid screens that pretend to sync. A backend holding health data brings PDPL storage and transfer duties. Abdullah plans to build this himself.

**A paywall or ads.** These are the clearest triggers of complaints in the evidence, and Miqdar's freedom from them is a selling point.

**Depth-sensor volume estimates and body or face scans.** The scale measures what a phone depth sensor can only estimate, and the spatial resolution of mobile LiDAR "is often insufficient for dietary assessment" ([Vinod & Zhu](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.05078)).

## Conclusion

The research changes the question. Miqdar does not need to catch up with MyFitnessPal's feature list. Most of that list is either already in Miqdar for free or is something users rate low. The scale has moved the accuracy problem: portion size, the error that sinks every photo app, is mostly gone. What remains is the per-100 g value of oily home dishes and whether Gemini names the dish right. That makes cooking context, the SFDA tables and the accuracy test worth more than any extra logging method. The one feature that only a scale can do, and that matches a documented Gulf need, is weighing a shared platter before and after the meal.

The biggest open risk is not a missing feature. No one has yet measured how accurate Gemini is when it is given real grams on Gulf food, so nobody knows the error Miqdar actually ships. A formal test against weighed reference meals and the SFDA's lab values would be new evidence. It would turn the demo from "it works" into "it is this accurate", and it is the strongest answer to Qalzy, which has published no accuracy figures at all.
